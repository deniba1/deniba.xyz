How to tell if a Roblox account is an alt: a 12-point checklist

By Deni · Published 25 October 2026 · 5 min read

You do not need a tool to judge a Roblox profile. Everything that matters is on the public profile page, and an experienced moderator reads it in a minute. This is the checklist they are running in their head, written down, with the reason each item matters and the ways it can mislead you.

Open the profile at roblox.com/users/<id>/profile, or search the username on Roblox. Then work down the list. Count how many items point to "throwaway". One or two mean nothing. Eight or nine on the same profile describe an account nobody intends to keep.

1. Join date

The single strongest signal. Roblox shows the join date in the profile's About section. An account created this week has had no time to be anything. An account from 2017 has been kept for the better part of a decade, and nobody keeps a throwaway that long. If the join date is hidden or you only have a user ID, the guide on account age and user IDs shows how to estimate it anyway.

Where it misleads: a new account is new whether it is a first account or a tenth. On day one you cannot tell, and should not pretend to.

2. Friends

Zero friends is the classic alt tell. Friends take two people agreeing, and an account made to be thrown away has nobody to agree with. Fifty or more friends is strong evidence of a main. The quality matters as much as the count: click a few. Are they established accounts who are friends with each other, or are they ten empty profiles created the same week? A ring of new accounts that know only each other is a bot farm or one person's collection of alts.

3. Followers and following

Followers come from being seen: in a game, in a group, on a video. Zero followers on an account that claims to be a known player is odd. Following nobody is a smaller tell, but it fits the pattern of an account that was made and then never explored.

4. Groups

Joining a group is free and one click, so an account in zero groups has not even done the easy things. Membership of ten or more groups, or ownership of one, points to a main. Look at which groups: a trading group, a clan, a fan group for a game, all suggest real interests. Twenty "free Robux" groups suggest a bot.

5. Roblox badges

These are the official badges Roblox itself awards, listed under Roblox Badges on the profile: Friendship for making friends, Homestead and Bricksmith for visits to a place you built, Welcome To The Club for Premium, Veteran for a year on the platform, and a few others. A main picks up two or three without trying. An account with none has done almost nothing.

6. Game badges

Games award their own badges for playing: finishing a level, finding a secret, reaching a milestone. Hundreds of these, earned on many different days, is a record of an account being played. Zero means it never has been. The trap here is the badge walk: games that award dozens of badges for walking across a room, which a farmer can use to pad an empty account with hundreds of badges in an evening. Look at when badges were earned. Ten in ten minutes is farming; ten across ten weeks is playing. The guide on badge walks explains how to tell.

7. Favorites

Favoriting a game is one click, costs nothing, and is how players bookmark what they enjoy. Zero favorites fits an account that has not played anything. Fifteen or more fits a main.

8. Inventory and limiteds

Open the inventory, if it is public. Limited items are the clearest wealth signal on Roblox: they cost real Robux, hold value, and are tracked by Recent Average Price. Almost no throwaway owns one. Paid clothing and accessories tell a softer version of the same story. Ignore free items, since any new account can grab a hundred of them in a minute. If the inventory is private, note that and move on; alts hide empty inventories, but plenty of mains hide full ones. The guide on limiteds and RAP has the detail.

9. Avatar

The default avatar, or one wearing a single free hat, is the look of an account made in a hurry. A main has usually spent some Robux and some time here. Not always: some long-time players like the default look, and some accounts are deliberately plain. Weigh it lightly.

10. Username

Roblox usernames are unique, so later accounts end up with digits tacked on: CoolPlayer was taken, CoolPlayer48213 was not. A run of several digits at the end is weak evidence of a hastily made account and stronger evidence of a script-generated one. Words like alt, backup, 2nd or spare in the name are the owner telling you outright. A username that closely matches a banned player's name with extra characters is the most useful clue of all for a moderator.

11. Display name and description

Display names can be changed freely, so a display name still identical to the username means the owner never bothered. An empty About section says the same. A description that mentions "my main" or "alt of" settles the question. Past usernames, shown on the profile, are the opposite: changing a username costs Robux, and nobody spends it on a throwaway.

12. Created places

Every account gets a default place, which means nothing. A published game with real visits means someone built something and other people played it. That is a main.

Putting it together

CountRead it asWhat to do
0–3MainTreat as a normal account.
4–6UnclearProbably quiet or new. Watch behaviour, not the profile.
7–9Likely throwawayApply whatever your rules say about new accounts. Do not accuse.
10–12Almost certainly throwawayIf the name or friends link it to a banned player, act on that link.

The count is a way to be consistent, not a verdict. A seven-year-old account that has gone quiet can tick six of these boxes and still be someone's only account. A careful ban evader can tick two. What the checklist does is stop you from being swayed by a single detail, in either direction, and make you look at the same twelve things every time, so that two moderators looking at the same profile reach the same answer.
