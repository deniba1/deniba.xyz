What is a Roblox alt account?

By Deni · Published 25 October 2026 · 5 min read

An alt, short for alternate account, is any Roblox account that belongs to someone who already has another one. Most alts are harmless. The ones people worry about are made to get around a ban, to hide who is behind an account, or to tilt a game or a trade. Telling the two groups apart is the whole problem.

Why alts are so easy to make

Creating a Roblox account takes under a minute. You pick a username, enter a birthday and a password, and you are in. An email address is optional. There is no phone verification, no payment, and nothing stops the same person coming back and doing it again with a different name. Roblox itself does not forbid holding several accounts; its rules forbid what people do with them, such as evading a ban or deceiving other players.

The result is that alts are everywhere. In a game with a million monthly players, a meaningful share of the accounts in any server will belong to people who also have a main. That is not a problem in itself. A quiet second account that someone uses to test their own game is an alt. So is the account a parent made to play alongside their child.

The kinds of alt you will meet

Test and build alts
Developers keep a second account to see their game as a new player sees it, or to test team features, trades and permissions. These are usually old, sparse and completely innocent.
Storage and trading alts
Traders park limited items on a second account, or keep one account for trading and another for playing so that trade requests do not interrupt them. These alts often hold valuable inventories and have Premium, which is required to trade, so they do not look like throwaways at all.
Fresh-start alts
Someone wants a new username, a clean friends list, or to play without their old group knowing. These accounts are new but behave like mains: the owner adds friends, buys clothes, favorites games and plays normally.
Ban-evasion alts
Made minutes after a ban to get back into a game, group or server. Brand new, default avatar, no friends, no badges, often a name that is the old one with digits added. This is what moderators mean when they say "alt", and it is against Roblox's rules as well as the rules of whatever they were banned from.
Troll and burner alts
Made to say or do things the owner does not want attached to their main. Same shape as a ban-evasion alt, and often discarded after one session.
Bot and farm alts
Made in bulk by scripts to inflate a game's visits, farm badges, spam, or carry out scams. Names are often random letters followed by a run of digits, created seconds apart, and the accounts are friends only with each other.

What a throwaway looks like

The accounts in the last three categories share a shape, because their owners put nothing into them. They were created recently. They have zero or one or two friends, no followers, and belong to no groups. They have never earned a badge in any game and never favorited one. The avatar is the default, or wears one free item. The inventory is empty or hidden. The display name was never set, so it still matches the username. The username ends in digits, because the owner's first few choices were taken, or because a script generated it.

None of these facts is damning on its own. Taken together on a single profile they describe an account nobody intends to keep. The guide on spotting an alt by hand turns this into a checklist you can run on any profile in about a minute.

What a main looks like

Mains accumulate. Years of join date. Dozens or hundreds of friends who are themselves established accounts. Badges from games played across many different days. Favorites, groups, a customised avatar, some paid clothing, maybe a limited item or two, maybe a published place with a few hundred visits. A username that has been changed at some point, which costs Robux and signals attachment. None of this can be faked quickly, and that is precisely why it is evidence.

Why the two are hard to tell apart on day one

A genuinely new player and a ban-evading alt look the same on the day they are created, because both have done nothing yet. The difference shows up over the following weeks: the new player's profile fills in, the alt's does not. Any check that runs on a one-day-old account is therefore guessing, and the honest answer for a very young account is "too early to tell", not "alt".

At the other end, a determined person can dress an alt up: buy a few items, add friends from their main's circle, play for a week. The account then scores like a quiet main. Profile checks catch the lazy majority, not the careful minority, and that is still worth a lot, because the lazy majority is most of the problem.

What Roblox does about it

Roblox's Community Standards prohibit circumventing moderation, and Roblox does act on ban evasion at the platform level. Since 2024, game developers have also had a ban API that can, at the developer's option, apply a ban to the other accounts Roblox links to the same person. That link uses data only Roblox has, such as device and network information, which is exactly the data no public tool can see. For groups, Discord servers and anything outside a single game, there is no such mechanism, which is why moderators fall back on profile checks.

A fair way to think about alts

Holding an alt is not an offence. Being new is not an offence. The question worth asking is never "is this an alt?" but "is this account here to break a rule it was already banned for?". Profile signals can raise that suspicion; only behaviour can confirm it. Good moderation uses the signals to decide where to look, and uses what the account actually does to decide what to do. The guide for group and game owners goes into how to write rules that work that way.
