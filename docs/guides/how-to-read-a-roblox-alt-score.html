How to read a Roblox alt score without getting it wrong

By Deni · Published 25 October 2026 · 5 min read

An alt score compresses a dozen facts about a profile into one number between 0 and 100. That makes it quick to read and easy to over-read. This guide explains how the number is built, which signals move it most, the situations where it is wrong, and what to actually do with it.

How the number is built

Every account starts at 35. Each signal the checker finds adds points (toward "alt") or subtracts them (toward "main"), and the result is clamped to the range 0 to 100. Positive and negative signals are listed side by side under "Why this score", largest first, so the number is never a black box: you can add it up yourself.

ScoreVerdictWhat it means
70–100Likely altMost signals point to a throwaway or secondary account.
50–69Possibly altMixed signals; leans toward an alt or a very inactive account.
30–49Probably mainLooks like a real, moderately active account.
0–29Likely mainStrong history and activity for a primary account.

Notice that the starting point, 35, is already inside "Probably main". An account about which nothing is known leans slightly toward main, because most accounts are mains. It takes evidence to move it into alt territory.

The signals that matter most

A few signals dominate, and knowing them tells you most of what the score will say before you read it.

SignalPointsWhy so heavy

The rest are smaller: followers, groups and whether an owned group has real members, favorites, avatar, paid wearables and how long ago the oldest item was picked up, created places and their visits, badges spread across many games and how far back the first one goes, display name, description, digits or a year at the end of the username, usernames with the shape of a machine-made name, past username changes. A few cross-check each other: hundreds of followers on an account that has never played anything, or following hundreds of accounts with no followers, are bot patterns. Descriptions that mention a ban, a test account, or buying and selling accounts count toward alt. Each is worth a few points. They matter in aggregate, which is the point: an account that trips eight small signals in the same direction is describing itself.

A worked example

An account created 12 days ago (+22), with no friends (+12), no followers (+5), no groups (+6), no Roblox badges (+8), no player badges (+12), no favorites (+6), a default avatar (+6), an empty inventory (+6), a username ending in five digits (+4), display name never set (+2) and no description (+3) starts at 35 and ends at 100 after clamping: Likely alt. Every one of those facts is also true of a child who made an account this morning and has not played yet. The score is right that the profile looks like a throwaway, and silent on whether it is one. That is the single most important thing to understand about it.

Now take an account from 2016 (−12) with 80 friends (−10), 40 followers (−4), in 12 groups (−6), with 4 Roblox badges (−6), 250 badges earned over 90 days (−12 and −4), 30 favorites (−5), a dozen paid wearables (−3), a customised avatar (−3) and a description (−2). It lands at 0: Likely main. It could still be somebody's second account. It is not a throwaway, and for almost every purpose that is the question.

Where the score is misleading

Brand-new genuine players
The biggest source of false alarms. Roblox gains far more new players every day than it bans, so most one-week -old accounts with nothing on them are exactly what they look like: new. Read a high score on a very young account as "too early to tell".
Quiet, private or returning players
Someone who plays one game with the same three friends, keeps their inventory private, never joins groups and ignores badges can sit in "Possibly alt" for years. Age is their defence, and the checker weights it heavily, but a sparse profile will never score as low as a busy one.
Non-social play styles
Builders who live in Studio, players who only do solo obbies, and people who use Roblox for a single experience often have thin social stats and strong other signals. Look at the created places and badge days before deciding.
Dressed-up alts
The main source of false negatives. A month of aging, a few friends from the owner's circle, a bought hat and an evening of play move an alt into "Probably main". Profile checks catch the lazy majority; behaviour catches the rest.
Bought accounts
An old, rich account that changed hands scores as a main because it is one; only the owner changed. Age and inventory describe the account's history, not its current driver.
Checks that were skipped
Roblox rate-limits some of its endpoints, and when a check cannot run the result lists it under "Not checked". A score computed without the badge check or the inventory check is less reliable in the direction of "alt", because those are mostly negative signals. Re-run the lookup a minute later if they matter.

The deep check moves the score, and why

A deep check reads the account's friends: how old they are, estimated from their user IDs, whether their own profiles have anything on them, and whether they are friends with each other. Friends who are mostly new, who were created the same week as the account, who have empty profiles, or who do not know each other all push toward alt (+12, +10, +8 and +6 respectively). Friends who are long-standing, have real profiles and know each other pull the other way (−8, −4, −8). Two or more banned friends among those read, or friends whose usernames are near-copies of the account's own, add more. A main sits in a circle of people; a farm of alts sits in a circle of itself.

What to do with a result

  • Read the signals, not the number. The number is a sort key. The list under it is the finding.
  • Check the age first. Under a month old, the score mostly measures youth. Over three years, the score mostly measures neglect. Neither is a crime.
  • Look for the link, not the alt. For moderation, what matters is whether this account is a banned person returning. A matching name, shared friends or the same behaviour is evidence of that. A score is not.
  • Never accuse on a score. Say "new accounts can't do X yet", never "you're an alt". The first is a rule; the second is a guess said out loud.
  • Keep your rules about what you can see. Account age, behaviour, and demonstrable links to a banned account are things you can point to. "Scored 78" is not a reason anyone has to accept.
